Understanding Power Grid Voltage Levels: From 11 kV to 765 kV

How Solar & Renewable-Energy Projects Connect to the National Grid

Why Grid Voltage Matters in Renewable Energy

When developing a solar power project, one question is often overlooked: “Where will the electricity actually go after it is generated?”

Finding suitable land and calculating solar potential are only the beginning. For a successful renewable-energy project, power evacuation and grid connection can be just as important as the land itself.

A solar project may generate 5 MW, 50 MW, 100 MW or even 500 MW, but that power must ultimately travel through an electrical network to reach a customer, distribution system or the national transmission grid.

This is where voltage levels such as 11 kV, 33 kV, 66 kV, 110 kV, 132 kV, 220 kV, 400 kV and 765 kV become important.

The simplest way to understand voltage is to think of the electricity network as a system of roads: 11 kV is like a local road, 33 kV a larger regional road, 66/110/132 kV a major highway, 220 kV an expressway, 400 kV a national-scale power highway and 765 kV a high-capacity interstate power corridor.

What Does Voltage Actually Mean?

A simple analogy is water flowing through a pipe.

Voltage = pressure

Current = quantity of flow

Transmission line = pipe

Transformer = pressure-changing equipment

If we want to move a large amount of electricity over a long distance, increasing the voltage allows the same power to be transmitted with significantly lower current.

For a simplified three-phase system: Power ≈ √3 × Voltage × Current.

Therefore, for approximately the same power: Higher voltage → lower current. Lower current generally means lower conductor losses and less heating for a given transmission arrangement.

The Journey of Electricity From a 100 MW Solar Plant

Consider a hypothetical 100 MW solar power project. The electricity does not simply travel directly from the solar panels to a 220 kV or 400 kV transmission line. It normally passes through stages of electrical collection and transformation.

Simplified arrangement: Solar Panels → Inverters → 11/33 kV Collection → Pooling Substation → 132/220/400 kV → Grid.

Importantly, a 100 MW project does not necessarily need to use every voltage level. The appropriate connection voltage depends on location, project capacity, existing network, available transmission capacity, distance, system studies and the requirements of the relevant transmission/distribution utility.

11 kV — Local Electrical Network

Solar modules produce DC electricity. Inverters convert this into AC electricity, and transformers step the voltage up to a suitable collection voltage. For smaller blocks within a solar project, 11 kV can be used as an internal or local collection level. Think of 11 kV as a local road connecting individual areas of a solar development.

33 kV — Solar Plant Collection Highway

33 kV is widely used for collecting power from multiple solar blocks and connecting renewable projects to suitable grid infrastructure. A large solar project may have several generation blocks feeding a 33 kV collection system. For a large project, 33 kV is often an important internal collection level rather than necessarily the final long-distance transmission voltage.

66 kV — Regional Transmission

66 kV sits above typical 33 kV distribution/collection systems and can be used for regional transmission and grid interconnection in areas where such infrastructure exists. A project could potentially be connected through 33 kV → 66 kV → Grid. However, the existence of a nearby 66 kV substation does not automatically mean that it can accept a large solar project.

110 kV — Larger Regional Grid

110 kV is another intermediate transmission level used in some parts of the electricity network. A renewable project may potentially connect to a 110 kV system where the local transmission network supports it. The exact connection must be established through technical and regulatory assessment.

132 kV — Major Grid Connection

132 kV is an important transmission voltage in many parts of India. A 100 MW solar project could potentially be connected to a suitable 132 kV network where sufficient capacity exists. A simplified arrangement is: 100 MW Solar → 33 kV Collection → 33/132 kV Pooling Substation → 132 kV Transmission Line → 132 kV Grid Substation.

220 kV — Large Renewable-Energy Evacuation

As renewable projects become larger, 220 kV becomes increasingly important. A 100 MW project with future development potential of 300 MW or 500 MW may evaluate a 220 kV grid connection. A possible strategy is 33 kV collection → 33/220 kV pooling substation → 220 kV transmission line → 220 kV grid substation.

400 kV — Large Renewable-Energy Corridors

400 kV transmission systems are designed for bulk power transfer and form an important part of India's high-voltage transmission network. For a larger renewable-energy development, multiple solar projects can potentially feed a pooling substation, followed by 220/400 kV transformation and connection to the 400 kV transmission network.

765 kV — High-Capacity Transmission Highway

765 kV is one of the highest AC transmission voltage levels used extensively in India's transmission system. It is designed for very large-scale bulk power transfer over long distances. A 100 MW solar project would not normally require a dedicated 765 kV connection simply because it generates 100 MW. The 765 kV system becomes particularly relevant when very large quantities of generation are aggregated and transmitted over long distances.

Why Does Higher Voltage Help?

Consider approximately 100 MW of power. Using a simplified three-phase calculation, the approximate current required is:

VoltageApprox. Current for 100 MW*
11 kV5,249 A
33 kV1,749 A
66 kV875 A
110 kV525 A
132 kV437 A
220 kV262 A
400 kV144 A
765 kV75 A

*Illustrative calculation assuming unity power factor and ignoring practical system losses and other engineering factors.

This demonstrates the basic principle: Same power + higher voltage = lower current. Lower current can significantly reduce resistive losses for a given conductor arrangement. That is why high-voltage networks are essential for moving large quantities of electricity over long distances.

Does a 33 kV Substation Automatically Accept 100 MW?

No. This is one of the most important misconceptions in renewable-energy development.

A substation may have a particular transformer rating, but that does not mean the same amount of renewable generation is automatically available for new connectivity.

Therefore: The nearest substation is not always the best substation.

What If the Nearby Substation Does Not Have Enough Capacity?

A project does not necessarily have to be abandoned. Possible strategies include:

Connect to another nearby, stronger substation.

Build a pooling substation for multiple projects.

Develop a dedicated evacuation line, subject to applicable approvals.

Connect at a higher voltage such as 132/220/400 kV where technically justified.

Share common infrastructure with other developers where permitted and commercially feasible.

STU, CTU and ISTS — Who Controls the Grid?

STU — State Transmission Utility

The State Transmission Utility manages the state's transmission network and associated planning and operations within the applicable state framework. For a state-level renewable project, the STU can be a critical authority in determining the appropriate transmission connection and system requirements.

CTU — Central Transmission Utility

The Central Transmission Utility performs central functions associated with the Inter-State Transmission System, including connectivity and transmission planning functions under the applicable regulatory framework.

ISTS — Inter-State Transmission System

ISTS is the high-voltage transmission network used for transferring electricity between states and across the interconnected national system. It becomes particularly important for large renewable projects targeting interstate power transfer, C&I supply, group captive structures, open access and large PPAs, subject to applicable regulations.

The National Grid Is Not One Giant Substation

When people say, “We want to connect to the National Grid,” it does not mean there is one single national-grid substation.

India has an interconnected transmission network made up of large numbers of transmission lines and substations operating at different voltage levels.

The practical question is: Which suitable transmission node can this project connect to, and what evacuation infrastructure is required to reach it?

For a large renewable project, this could involve: Solar Plant → Pooling Substation → 220/400 kV → ISTS → National Interconnected Grid.

The real opportunity for a renewable-energy developer is not simply “Build a 100 MW solar plant.” It is to identify a strong renewable-energy development zone and build the generation, evacuation and commercial ecosystem around it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between 33 kV and 220 kV?

33 kV is generally used for lower-voltage collection/distribution and smaller regional networks, while 220 kV is a higher-voltage transmission level suitable for much larger power transfers. Actual suitability depends on the local network.

Can a 100 MW solar project connect directly to 33 kV?

It may be technically possible in some circumstances, but it cannot be assumed. The utility must assess the network's ability to accept the proposed generation.

Is 220 kV always better than 132 kV?

Not necessarily. A higher voltage may provide greater transmission capability, but it can also require more expensive infrastructure. The best voltage balances technical reliability and economics.

Can 500 MW be connected to 220 kV?

It may be possible in some network configurations, but it depends heavily on the specific system, line configuration, distance, transformer capacity, N-1 requirements, fault levels and utility study. There is no universal rule that 220 kV equals a particular MW limit.

Does a 400 kV substation mean 500 MW is available?

No. A 400 kV substation's voltage rating does not represent spare generation capacity. Actual connectivity depends on existing and committed flows, transformation capacity, network constraints and system studies.

What is a pooling substation?

A pooling substation collects electricity from one or more generating projects and combines it before transferring the power to a larger transmission network. It is particularly useful for large renewable-energy clusters.

Why can't we simply connect to the nearest substation?

Because the nearest substation may not have sufficient capacity or may have network constraints. A stronger substation farther away may provide a better long-term evacuation solution.

What is ISTS?

ISTS stands for Inter-State Transmission System. It is the high-voltage transmission network used for transferring electricity across state boundaries and forms an important part of India's interconnected transmission system.

What is the difference between STU and CTU?

The STU deals primarily with the state transmission system, while the CTU performs central functions relating to the inter-state transmission system. The applicable authority depends on the project's location, voltage, connectivity point and commercial structure.

Does higher voltage always mean lower cost?

No. Higher voltage can reduce transmission losses and enable larger power transfers, but substations, transformers, breakers, towers and transmission lines can be more expensive. The optimum solution balances CAPEX, losses, reliability, distance, project capacity and future expansion.

Can a solar project move from 33 kV directly to 400 kV?

Yes, through appropriate transformation and pooling infrastructure, if the project and grid study justify it. The project does not have to physically pass through every voltage level.

Can a 100 MW project eventually become a 500 MW project?

Potentially, yes. But the land, grid connectivity, evacuation system, substation design, transmission capacity and approvals must all support expansion.

What should I check before buying land for a large solar project?

At minimum: land title, solar resource, nearest grid nodes, voltage, available/allocable connectivity, existing and committed generation, evacuation route, transmission cost, buyer/offtake and regulatory approvals.

What is the most important lesson for a renewable-energy developer?

Don't develop the solar plant first and solve the grid later. Develop the land, grid and evacuation strategy together.

Conclusion

Voltage levels are essentially different levels of the electricity highway: 11 kV → 33 kV → 66 kV → 110 kV → 132 kV → 220 kV → 400 kV → 765 kV.

As voltage increases, the same amount of power can generally be transmitted with lower current, making high-voltage networks suitable for large-scale and long-distance power transfer.

For a renewable-energy developer, however, the highest voltage is not automatically the best answer.

The right question is: “What is the most technically reliable and commercially economical path to evacuate the project's power?”

For a 100–500 MW renewable project, that means looking beyond the nearest substation and evaluating the entire chain:

Land → Solar Capacity → Collection System → Pooling Substation → Transmission Line → Grid Connectivity → Buyer → Revenue

That is the foundation of scalable renewable-energy development.
